## Reading Skills

Reading is the skill of decoding written symbols — letters, punctuation, and spaces — and turning them into meaning inside our heads. It looks passive, but it isn't: a good reader is always predicting, questioning, and connecting what's on the page to what they already know.

### Two Core Sub-Skills

- **Skimming** — reading quickly to get the *overall* idea of a passage. You are not hunting for a detail; you just want to know "what is this about?"
- **Scanning** — searching a passage for one *specific* piece of information, such as a date, a name, or a number, without reading every word.

A good habit is to skim first (get the whole picture) and then scan (go back for the parts you need) — never the other way round.

### How to Read a Passage Effectively

1. Read silently, calmly, and carefully — don't rush.
2. Move from the whole passage to its parts, not the other way round.
3. Read complete sentences or clauses at a time instead of picking out stray words.
4. If a word is unfamiliar, guess its meaning from the context instead of panicking.
5. Underline or note important names, dates, definitions, and ideas as you read.
6. Pay attention to **discourse markers** (words like *however, therefore, for example, in addition, on the other hand*) — they are signposts that show how ideas connect and what the writer's attitude is.
7. Don't skip words or lines; you might miss information a question depends on.
9. Questions usually follow the order in which information appears in the passage.
10. Read every question carefully before hunting for its answer — questions may ask for facts, reasons, causes, effects, or your own inference.
11. Base every answer strictly on the passage; don't add your own opinion unless asked.

### Common Types of Comprehension Questions

| Type | What You Do |
|---|---|
| Multiple Choice | Pick the correct option from those given |
| Fill in the Blanks | Complete a sentence using information from the passage |
| True/False | Judge a statement against the passage |
| Matching | Match items in one column with items in another |
| Very Short Answer | Answer in one word or a short phrase |
| Short Answer | Answer in one or two full sentences |
| Sentence Ordering | Arrange jumbled sentences in the order they appear in the passage |
| Mind Maps / Flow Charts | Fill missing information into a diagram |

### Useful Literary Devices to Recognise While Reading

These often appear in comprehension passages and poems, so knowing them helps you answer "which figure of speech is used here?" questions.

- **Simile** — a direct comparison using *like, as,* or *than* — e.g., "her voice was like a bell."
- **Metaphor** — calling one thing another, without *like/as* — e.g., "the classroom was a zoo."
- **Personification** — giving human qualities to non-human things — e.g., "the wind whispered."
- **Alliteration** — repeating the first sound of nearby words — e.g., "she sells sea shells."
- **Onomatopoeia** — a word that imitates a sound — e.g., *buzz, chirp, crash*.
- **Hyperbole** — a deliberate exaggeration — e.g., "I've told you a million times."
- **Irony** — when what happens is the opposite of what is expected.
- **Oxymoron** — two contradictory words placed together — e.g., "deafening silence."
- **Imagery** — descriptive language that creates a picture using our five senses.

## Notice Writing

A **notice** is a short, formal piece of writing used by a school, club, or organisation to give information to a group of people.

### Parts of a Notice

- Name of the organisation — in capital letters, centred
- The word **NOTICE** — in capital letters, centred
- Date of issuing the notice — aligned left
- A short, catchy heading that hints at the message — centred
- Body — the actual information: what, when, where, who, and any other essential detail
- Issuing authority — signature, name, and designation, aligned left

### Format

```
              NAME OF THE ORGANISATION
                      NOTICE
Date                                          Heading

................. BODY OF THE NOTICE .................

Signature
NAME
(Designation)
```

### Tips for an Effective Notice

- Keep your purpose and your audience clearly in mind.
- Give complete, correct information — don't leave gaps.
- Use short, simple, formal sentences.
- Capitalise important details (names, events, dates).
- Enclose the notice in a box.
- Stick to the word limit (usually around 50 words) — don't pad it with unnecessary detail.
- Avoid personal opinions, slang, contractions, and personal pronouns ("I", "we") in the body.
- Prefer passive constructions ("is requested to", "are informed") over direct commands.

### Sample Notice

```
        SACRED HEART CONVENT SCHOOL, SOLAN
                    NOTICE
April 15, 20XX
                LUNCH BOX LOST

A brand-new red lunch box with a white lid and a Spider-Man
picture was lost in the playground on April 13 (Saturday).
Whoever finds it is requested to return it to the undersigned.
A suitable reward is promised.

Geetika Kaushik
GEETIKA KAUSHIK
Roll No. 24, VIII A
```

## Message Writing

A **message** is a short, informal note that passes on information or instructions to someone who isn't available to take a call or conversation directly. It is usually based on a telephone conversation between a caller and whoever answered.

### Parts of a Message

- The word **MESSAGE**
- Date and time
- Name of the person the message is for
- The message itself, including who called
- The writer's name

### Format

```
                     MESSAGE
Date
Time
(Salutation)

......... Message to be conveyed .........

Writer's Name
```

### Steps

1. Note down the key facts first: who called, who it's for, what they said, and what the receiver needs to do.
2. Write "Message", then the date and time.
3. Add a brief salutation (*Dear Papa*, *Mom*, etc.).
4. Write the body — name the caller, state the message clearly, and include any date/time/place details.
5. Sign off with your own name.
6. Enclose the message in a box.

### Sample

```
                     MESSAGE
April 24, 20XX
7:00 a.m.
Papa

Your colleague, Mr. Ajeet Sood, called to say that your
meeting with the Japanese delegate, earlier at 9:30 at Kings
Hotel, has been postponed by two hours to 11:30 a.m. Please
reach the office directly at 9:30 instead of going to the hotel.

Abhimanyu
```

**Useful phrases:** "...rang up to inform you that...", "You are supposed to reach/bring/call...", "The next... will be at... on..."

## Bio-Sketch (Biographical Sketch)

A **bio-sketch** is a brief, formal write-up of a person's life — their birth, family, education, career, and achievements — usually written in about 100 words from notes provided to you.

### Points to Cover

Date and place of birth · parents' names and occupation · early schooling · educational qualifications · hobbies/interests · notable achievements (with dates) · profession · personal life · social status · awards and honours · date and cause of death (if applicable)

### Sample (condensed)

> Lata Mangeshkar, one of India's greatest playback singers, was born on 28 September 1929 in Indore to Pandit Deenanath Mangeshkar, a classical singer and theatre actor, and Shevanti Mangeshkar. The eldest of five siblings, she began performing in her father's plays at the age of five and recorded her first song at thirteen. Over her career she won three National Film Awards and four Filmfare Best Female Playback awards, and was honoured with the Padma Bhushan (1969), Padma Vibhushan (1999), and the Bharat Ratna (2001). Her melodious voice earned her titles such as "Queen of Melody" and "Nightingale of India."

Notice how the sample moves in order: birth → family → early career → achievements → honours — that same top-to-bottom order works for any bio-sketch you're asked to write.

## Diary Entry

A diary entry is a **personal, informal** record of your day-to-day experiences, thoughts, and feelings — meant only for the writer's own eyes.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Style | Personal, informal, conversational |
| Length | Usually 100–150 words for exams |
| Language | First person (I, me, my); can include exclamations |
| Tenses | Simple past for what happened; present for general feelings; present perfect for very recent events; simple future for plans |
| Salutation | "Dear Diary" or a name you've given your diary |
| Sign-off | "Bye", "Good night", "See you tomorrow" |

### Format

```
Day, Date
Time

Dear Diary,
......... Body of the entry .........

(Sign-off)
Name
```

### Making a Diary Entry Effective

- Describe your feelings in detail, not just the bare event.
- Use varied, expressive vocabulary (calm, thrilled, furious, embarrassed...).
- Use the correct tense for past events versus future plans.
- Stay on topic and keep language natural, not stiff or formal.

### Sample (condensed)

> Tuesday, 30 March, 20XX — 10:00 p.m.
> Dear Diary,
> It was the happiest day of my life! My final exam results were declared today, and I topped the class with 99%. My classmates cheered, my teacher patted my back, and my parents were overjoyed. We celebrated with sweets and a family dinner. I hope I can keep this up in the years ahead.
> Good night!
> — Advika

## Factual Description

A factual description is a **formal, objective** account of a person, object, place, or event, usually written as a single well-organised paragraph (about 80–100 words for exams).

### Golden Rules

- Describe facts, not opinions — stay objective and realistic.
- Cover one aspect at a time; don't jump between ideas.
- Use formal language and varied vocabulary; avoid repeating words.

### Describing People

Cover: physical traits (age, height, build, face) → personality traits (kind, talkative, disciplined...) → occupation → hobbies → what others think of them.

> *Sample:* "Mr. Sharma is my favourite teacher. He is tall, well-built, and always smartly dressed. Soft-spoken and kind, he explains science concepts clearly and often cracks a witty joke to keep the class engaged. He enjoys reading and playing golf. Everyone in school admires him."

### Describing Objects

Cover: name → brand/model → shape, colour, material → parts and features → uses/capacity → price → your experience with it.

### Describing Places

Cover: name and location → size/area → surroundings → structure (entrance, layout, flooring, lighting) → importance or purpose.

### Describing Events

Cover: name of the event → date, time, venue → purpose → organisers and chief guests → what happened, in sequence → prizes or outcomes → overall impact.

## Informal Letter Writing

**Informal letters** are personal letters written to family, friends, or relatives — used to share news, invite someone, express feelings, apologise, or congratulate. There's no strict word limit, but every sentence should serve the purpose of the letter.

### Parts of an Informal Letter

1. **Sender's address** — top left corner (house/street/city/pin code)
2. **Date** — below the address
3. **Salutation** — *Dear Papa*, *Dear(est) Rahul* (never "Dear Friend")
4. **Body** — usually 3 paragraphs:
   - *Introduction* — a greeting, a reference to a previous letter, or the reason you're writing
   - *Main message* — the actual news or request, well organised
   - *Closure* — sign-off wishes, regards to family, an invitation to reply
5. **Subscription** — *Yours affectionately* (family), *Yours sincerely* (friends)
6. **Signature**

### Format

```
Sender's Address

Date

Dear (Name),

(Introduction)
(Main message)
(Closure)

With lots of love,
Yours sincerely/affectionately,
(Signature)
```

### Sample Opening Lines

"I hope this letter finds you in high spirits." · "I am extremely happy to know that..." · "I am sorry for replying late because..." · "You will be glad to know that..."

### Sample (condensed)

> House No. 45, Street No. 2, New Azad Nagar, Ludhiana
> December 2, 20XX
>
> Dear Abhijeet,
>
> I hope your exam preparation is going well! I'm writing to invite you to spend your winter break with me here in Ludhiana. We could visit the Rose Garden, watch a movie, and I've even planned a two-day trip to Chandigarh. My parents are excited to host you and my friends can't wait to meet you.
>
> Do let me know soon so we can plan everything. Give my regards to your parents.
>
> With lots of love,
> Yours sincerely,
> Anuraag

## Formal Letter Writing

**Formal letters** are official letters — applications, complaints, requests, or business correspondence — written in polite, impersonal language with no personal opinions.

### Common Types

Application to the Principal/class teacher/librarian · job applications · letters to a department head, bank manager, or editor · complaints · business letters

### Parts of a Formal Letter

1. **Sender's address** (skip this only when writing an *application to your own Principal*)
2. **Date**
3. **Receiver's address** — formal designation first, then organisation, then place, e.g.:
   ```
   The Principal
   St. Andrew Convent School
   Sector 32, Chandigarh
   ```
4. **Subject line** — one crisp line stating the purpose, e.g. *Subject: Request for Fee Concession*
5. **Salutation** — *Sir/Madam* or *Dear Sir/Madam*
6. **Body** (3 paragraphs):
   - *Introduction* — who you are and why you're writing
   - *Main message* — full details of the issue or request
   - *Closure* — what you want the reader to do, plus a thank-you line
7. **Subscription** — *Yours obediently* (to the Principal), *Yours faithfully/truly* (others)
8. **Signature**, and for a school application: roll number, class/section

### Format — Application to Principal

```
The Principal
(School Name and Address)

Date

Subject: (state purpose)

Sir/Madam,

I am (name), a student of Class ___, Section ___. (state reason for writing)

(Give full details of the issue/request)

(State clearly what you are requesting)

Thanking you,
Yours obediently,
(Signature)
Roll No.
Class/Section
```

### Sample (condensed)

> Subject: Request for exemption from Office Tests
>
> Sir,
>
> I am Akriti Singla, a student of Grade VIII A, Roll No. 7. I have been selected for the Theatre Round of a singing reality show, to be shot in Mumbai from August 5–10, which clashes with our scheduled Office Tests.
>
> I request you to kindly exempt me from these tests. I will be highly obliged.
>
> Thanking you,
> Yours obediently,
> Akriti Singla

## E-mail Writing

An **e-mail** is the electronic, digital version of a letter — fast, paperless, and able to reach multiple people at once with attachments. Like letters, e-mails can be formal or informal depending on the recipient.

### Parts of an E-mail

- **From** — sender's e-mail address
- **To** — receiver's e-mail address
- **Cc** (Carbon Copy) — others who should see the mail openly
- **Bcc** (Blind Carbon Copy) — others who receive it without the main recipient knowing
- **Subject** — a short, clear statement of purpose
- **Date**
- **Salutation, message, and subscription** — just like a letter

### Tips

- E-mail addresses are written in lowercase with no spaces.
- Keep the subject line clear and relevant.
- Use short paragraphs and simple, unambiguous language.
- Avoid slang, abbreviations, and unnecessary attachments.
- Sign off appropriately: *Regards* (informal) or *Yours sincerely/faithfully* with your name, and for formal mail, your designation and contact details.

### Sample (condensed)

```
From:    nehamehta6121@gmail.com
To:      ankitasharma@yahoo.com
Subject: Congratulations!
Date:    July 26, 20XX

Dear Ankita,

I was thrilled to see your name at the top of the merit list —
congratulations on securing the first rank in the board exams!
This is the result of all your hard work. When are you giving
me that treat?

With lots of love and best wishes,
Yours sincerely,
Neha
```

## Article Writing

An **article** is a piece of writing for a newspaper, magazine, or blog that expresses the writer's views or findings on a topic of public interest.

### Parts of an Article

1. **Title** — short, catchy, and relevant
2. **By-line** — the author's name, just below the title
3. **Introduction** — opens with a quote, question, statistic, or striking fact related to the topic
4. **Development** — the main body, usually 3–4 well-linked paragraphs covering different aspects (causes, effects, examples, both sides of an issue)
5. **Winding up** — briefly sums up the discussion
6. **Conclusion** — final view, along with suggestions or a call to action

### The Writing Process — remember **CODER**

- **C**ollect ideas relevant to the topic
- **O**rganise them in a logical sequence
- **D**raft the article, keeping the audience and word limit in mind
- **E**dit for spelling, grammar, and punctuation
- **R**evise and prepare the final version

### Tips

- Use formal, precise language — avoid slang and overly casual expressions.
- Give each paragraph one clear idea with a topic sentence and supporting details.
- Use cohesive devices (*however, moreover, as a result, on the other hand*) to link ideas smoothly.
- Add a relevant quotation if you can — it makes a strong opening.

### Sample Opening (condensed, from a published-style article)

> **Child Labour**
> *By Kalpana Srivastava*
> "There can be no keener revelation of a society's soul than the way in which it treats its children." — Nelson Mandela
>
> Children are a nation's future, yet child labour continues to rob millions of them of their childhood. Despite laws guaranteeing free education and banning child employment in hazardous work, the practice persists across agriculture, mining, and small-scale industries...

## Speech Writing

A **speech** is a formal talk delivered to an audience in order to inform, persuade, or inspire. Great speeches share three qualities:

- **Style** — carefully chosen, effective language
- **Substance** — a meaningful theme that appeals to the audience's values
- **Impact** — the power to actually change how listeners think or feel

### Layout of a Speech

1. **Introduction**
   - Greet the audience in order of seniority (Principal → teachers → fellow students → guests)
   - Introduce yourself — name, class, section
   - Open the topic with an anecdote, quotation, question, or striking fact
2. **Body**
   - Present your points in a logical sequence with examples, facts, or comparisons
   - Link ideas smoothly with cohesive devices
3. **Conclusion**
   - Sum up your message
   - End with a call to action, a thought-provoking question, or a hopeful note
   - Thank the audience formally

### Tips for an Effective Speech

- **Rule of 3** — points, examples, or adjectives grouped in threes are more memorable.
- **Imagery** — similes and vivid description keep listeners engaged.
- **Pronouns** — "we" builds unity, "you" speaks directly to the audience.
- **Rhythm** — repetition and alliteration make lines easier to remember.
- Keep language conversational; vary sentence types (statements, questions, exclamations).
- Keep an eye on the time limit, and rehearse to check your pacing.

### Useful Openings

"I am sure all of you will agree that..." · "There is no doubt that..." · "Do you know that...?" · "Let me share an example..."

### Sample (condensed)

> Honourable Principal, respected teachers, and my dear friends — a very good morning to all of you. I am here to speak about the importance of discipline in our lives.
>
> Discipline runs through everything around us — the earth follows its orbit, the seasons follow their cycle, and every institution, from schools to hospitals, functions because people follow rules. Without discipline, even personal life falls into chaos.
>
> Let us all resolve to bring greater discipline into our daily routines — in our studies, our habits, and our relationships. Thank you.

## Short Story Writing

A short story narrates an incident involving one or more characters — real or imaginary — and always has a **beginning, middle, and end**.

- **Beginning** — introduces characters, setting, and time; should immediately catch the reader's interest.
- **Middle** — develops the plot, introduces a problem or conflict, and builds curiosity about what happens next.
- **End** — brings the story to its climax and resolution, happy or otherwise, and usually delivers a message or moral.

### Key Elements

Plot · Characters · Time and setting · Dialogues · Actions and happenings

### Tips for Writing a Good Story

1. Choose a clear theme and decide on your main characters before you start.
2. Arrange events in a logical sequence — make a quick outline first.
3. Pick a suitable, interesting title.
4. Describe the setting vividly, but briefly.
5. Write mainly in the **past tense**; dialogue can be in present or future tense.
6. Use sequence markers (*first, then, later, eventually*) and connectors (*but, so, however*) to link events.
7. Use similes, metaphors, and other figures of speech to make the narration lively.
8. End with a clear moral or message.

### Three Common Ways a Story Task Is Set

- **From visuals** — build a story around a given picture or set of pictures.
- **From an opening line** — you're given the first line or two and must continue.
- **From an outline** — you're given a chain of hints (e.g., *king → loves gold → wishes for golden touch → daughter turns to gold → repents*) and must expand it into a full story.

### Sample (condensed) — "The Midas Touch"

> Long ago, King Midas of Greece loved gold above everything else — even his own daughter, whom he'd named Marigold. One day, a mysterious old man he had sheltered turned out to be an angel, who granted him a golden touch as a reward for his kindness. Overjoyed, Midas began turning everything he touched to gold — until his daughter ran to hug him and turned into a lifeless golden statue. Grief-stricken, Midas begged the angel to undo the spell, and Marigold was restored. Midas gave away his gold and never let greed rule him again.
> **Moral: Never let greed blind you to what truly matters.**

## Quick Revision Table

| Format | Style | Typical Length | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Notice | Formal | ~50 words | Boxed, passive voice, no opinions |
| Message | Informal | ~50 words | Based on a phone call, boxed |
| Bio-Sketch | Formal | ~100 words | Chronological life facts |
| Diary Entry | Informal | 100–150 words | First person, personal feelings |
| Factual Description | Formal | 80–100 words | Objective, one aspect at a time |
| Informal Letter | Semi-formal | No fixed limit | 3-part body, warm tone |
| Formal Letter | Formal | No fixed limit | Subject line, polite and precise |
| E-mail | Formal/Informal | Brief | Subject line + letter-style body |
| Article | Formal | 150–200 words | Title, by-line, structured paragraphs |
| Speech | Formal, spoken | 150–200 words | Greeting, body, thanks |
| Short Story | Narrative | 200–250 words | Beginning–Middle–End + moral |

_Always check your own school's prescribed word limits, since they can vary slightly from year to year._
