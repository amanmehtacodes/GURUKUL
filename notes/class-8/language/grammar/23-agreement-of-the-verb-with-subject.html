1.Spot the Error

Read the following sentences carefully.

  1. Ram and Sham is friends.
  2. I were not there.
  3. He like mangoes.
  4. All the boys is playing cricket.
  5. You is my friend.
  6. They has taken their meal.

THINK: Do you notice any abnormality in these sentences?

In each sentence given above, the verb does not agree with the subject in number or person. Now read the same sentences again.

Ram and Sham is friends.→Ram and Sham are friends.
I were not there.→I was not there.
He like mangoes.→He likes mangoes.
All the boys is playing cricket.→All the boys are playing cricket.
You is my friend.→You are my friend.
They has taken their meal.→They have taken their meal.

A finite verb agrees with its subject in number, person or tense. This is known as subject-verb agreement or agreement of the verb with its subject. Read the following rules and examples carefully to understand subject-verb agreement.

2.Rule 1 — Singular/Plural Subject and Verb

A singular subject takes a singular verb and a plural subject takes a plural verb.

  • The baby is sleeping.
  • The babies are sleeping.
  • He reads a book.
  • They read a book.
  • I am waiting for you.
  • We are waiting for you.

3.Rule 2 — Nouns/Pronouns Joined by 'And' (Separate Persons or Things)

When two or more nouns or pronouns are joined by 'and', and they represent two separate persons or things, the verb used should be plural.

  • Seeta and Geeta are going to market.
  • Gold and Platinum are precious metals.
  • You and he have taken your lunch.
  • Time and tide wait for none.

4.Rule 3 — Nouns Joined by 'And' (Single Person, Thing or Concept)

When two or more nouns or pronouns are joined by 'and', but they refer to or represent a single person, thing or concept, the verb should be singular.

  • Early to bed and early to rise is a good habit.
  • Rice and curry is my favourite meal.
  • The horse and carriage is ready.
  • My friend, guide and philosopher is my father.

5.Rule 4 — Two Singular Subjects Connected by 'Or'/'Nor'

When two or more singular subjects are connected by 'or' or 'nor' the verb should be singular.

  • Either Kunal or his friend has broken the window.
  • Neither the milkman or his son is ploughing the field.

6.Rule 5 — Singular and Plural Subject Connected by 'Or'/'Nor'

When a singular and a plural subject is connected by 'or' or 'nor', the plural subject is kept near the verb and the verb agrees with the plural subject.

  • Either Kunal or his friends have broken the window.
  • Neither the milkman or his sons are ploughing the field.

7.Rule 6 — Pronouns of Different Persons Connected by 'Or'/'Nor'

When the subjects joined by 'or' or 'nor' are pronouns of different persons, the verb should agree with the pronoun that is nearer the verb.

  • Either you or I am to blame.
  • Neither you nor he is mistaken.

9.Rule 7 — With, As Well As, Along With, No Less Than, Together With, Besides, And Not

When a Noun or a Pronoun is joined with another noun(s) or pronoun(s) using the words 'with', 'as well as', 'along with', 'no less than', 'together with', 'besides' or 'and not', the verb agrees with the one introduced first in the sentence.

  • The coach, with all the players, is practising in the field.
  • The players, as well as the coach, are practising in the field.
  • Abhinav, no less than his colleagues, is very honest.
  • Geetika, and not her sisters, has baked the cake.
  • I, as well as they, am sick of his behaviour.

10.Rule 8 — Each, Every, Either, Neither, Many a, Everyone...

The words such as 'each', 'every', 'either', 'neither', 'many a', 'everyone', 'everybody', 'somebody', 'one of', 'none of' etc. take a singular verb.

  • Each child was given a candy.
  • Everybody wants to be appreciated.
  • Somebody has stolen my purse.
  • One of my friends has settled in the USA.
  • Many a student was playing in the playground.

11.Rule 9 — Two Singular Subjects Joined by 'And', Qualified by 'Each'/'Every'

If two singular subjects joined by 'and' are qualified by 'each' or 'every', they take a singular verb.

  • Each hour and each minute is precious.
  • Every man and every woman desires happiness.

12.Rule 10 — Error of Proximity

When the subject is a phrase, the verb must agree with the subject proper instead of the word which is near the verb. The error of making the verb agree with the word near it is called the error of proximity.

  • The attitude of the officers is friendly.
  • A list of the candidates was prepared.

13.Rule 11 — Nouns That Look Singular but Take a Plural Verb

Some nouns which appear to be singular, but are treated as plural, take a plural verb.

  • The cattle are grazing in the field.
  • Six dozen cost rupees five hundred.
  • The people were shouting.

14.Rule 12 — Nouns That Look Plural but Take a Singular Verb

Some nouns appear to be plural, but they take a singular verb.

  • Politics is a dirty game.
  • The news of his arrival has come.
  • The wages of sin is death.
  • Mathematics is my favourite subject.

15.Rule 13 — Collective Nouns

Collective nouns like committee, government, jury, panel etc. take a singular verb when these nouns are used in the sense of a single unit or one whole; and a plural verb when the individuals making the whole are considered separately.

  • The jury was unanimous in its stand on the issue.
  • The jury were divided on this controversial issue.

16.Rule 14 — Plural Noun Used as the Name of a Singular Object or Unit

If a plural noun is used as a name of some singular object or collective unit, the verb that follows will be singular.

  • The Arabian Nights was translated by Richard Burton.
  • Gulliver's Travels is an interesting book.

17.Rule 15 — Plural Noun Denoting a Specific Quantity or Amount

When a plural noun denoting some specific quantity or amount is taken in the sense of a whole, the verb it takes should generally be singular. If it is not taken as a single whole, it takes a plural verb.

  • Eight months is a long time.
  • Fifty miles is a long distance.
  • Five thousand rupees is a great sum.
  • Five thousand rupees were paid to the workers.

18.Rule 16 — Two Nouns Joined by 'And', One Article or Two Articles

When two nouns joined by 'and' take a single article, they refer to the same person and take a singular verb; when they take two separate articles, they refer to two persons and take a plural verb.

  • The poet and novelist was honoured. (one person, both a poet and a novelist)
  • The poet and the novelist were honoured. (two different people)

19.Rule 17 — A Lot Of, A Plenty Of, Some Of, Most Of, Half Of, All The...

Certain phrases such as 'a lot of', 'a plenty of', 'some of', 'most of', 'half of', 'all the' etc. take a singular verb if followed by a singular noun; a plural verb if followed by a plural noun.

  • A lot of money was wasted on this project.
  • A lot of books were scattered in the room.

22.Quick Revision Checklist

  • A finite verb must agree with its subject in number and person — singular subject, singular verb; plural subject, plural verb.
  • 'And' usually needs a plural verb, unless the joined nouns name one single idea, dish, or role.
  • With 'or'/'nor', the verb agrees with the subject nearer to it — whether that's about number (singular/plural) or person (I/you/he).
  • Words like 'with', 'as well as', 'along with', 'together with' don't change the number of the main subject — the verb still agrees with whatever came first.
  • Each, every, either, neither, everyone, everybody, somebody, one of, none of — all take a singular verb.
  • Watch for the error of proximity: the verb agrees with the subject proper, not with whichever noun happens to sit right before it.
  • Some nouns look singular but are plural (cattle, people, police); others look plural but are singular (Mathematics, Politics, news).
  • Collective nouns (jury, committee, government) take a singular verb as one unit, plural when the members act separately.
  • A quantity or amount treated as a single whole (five thousand rupees, fifty miles) takes a singular verb; the same words describing separate units take a plural verb.
  • Phrases like 'a lot of', 'some of', 'most of', 'half of' take their verb's number from the noun that follows them.
