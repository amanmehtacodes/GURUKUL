1.Finite vs Non-Finite Verbs

Read the following forms of the verbs carefully:

  • Go, goes, went, gone, going, to go, having gone
  • Look, looks, looked, looked, looking, to look, having looked
  • Help, helps, helped, helped, helping, to help, having helped
  • Be, is, am, are, was, were, to be, been, being, having been
  • Do, does, did, done, doing, to do, having done
  • Has, have, had, to have, having, having had

Think: Why do we use different forms of verbs in sentences?

Look at the changes in the forms of verbs in the following sentences:

  1. He goes to school to study.
    They go to school to study. (Change in number of the subject)
  2. I will help you. (Determination)
    I may help you. (Possibility) (Change in expression/mood)
  3. They like swimming.
    They liked swimming. (Change in tense)
  4. He finds the door locked.
    I find the door locked. (Change in person of the subject)

The verbs that change according to change in tense, number, mood or person — 'goes'/'go', 'will'/'may', 'like'/'liked', 'finds'/'find' — are called finite verbs or finite forms of verbs. The other verbs in these sentences — 'to study', 'to help', 'swimming', 'locked' — don't change their forms according to change in tense, number, mood or person. These verbs are called non-finite forms of verbs.

3.Infinitives — Definition & Types

An infinitive is a type of non-finite form of verb that does not follow subject-verb agreement in the sentence. It is the first form of verb used with 'to' or without 'to'.

There are two types of Infinitives.

1. Infinitive with 'to': 'to + first form of verb'

  1. He goes to school to study.
  2. I wanted to play.
  3. They wish to join a new school.

In the above sentences, the verbs 'to study', 'to play' and 'to join' are infinitives with 'to'.

2. Infinitive without 'to' (Bare Infinitive): First form of verb

  1. Please let me go.
  2. I will help you.
  3. She does nothing but complain.

In the above sentences, the verbs 'go', 'help' and 'complain' are infinitives without 'to' or bare infinitives.

4.Infinitive with 'to' — Uses

Read the following examples carefully to understand the use of infinitive with 'to'.

  1. To err is human. (used as a subject)
  2. I want to go there. (used as an object)
  3. My ambition is to become a doctor. (used as a subject complement)
  4. It is easier to preach than to practise. (used after an adjective)
  5. He went to meet the President. (used to modify a verb)
  6. You are difficult to please. (used to modify an adjective)
  7. It is time to leave. (used to modify a noun)
  8. To cut the long story short, he cheated everybody. (used as a comment)
  9. He is about to resign. (used after a preposition)
  10. He was the first to arrive. (used as an adjunct)
  11. The patient is too weak to walk. (used with adverb 'too')
  12. I don't have money enough to buy a car. (used with adjective 'enough')

7.Infinitive without 'to' (Bare Infinitive)

Bare Infinitive is used in the following cases:

  1. After the auxiliary 'do', 'does' or 'did': (i) He does not like mangoes. (ii) They did not stay here. (iii) Do you have a spare pen?
  2. After modal auxiliaries: (i) I will help you. (ii) He may come tomorrow. (iii) You need not go there.
  3. After certain verbs such as: see, watch, hear, feel, let, make, help, bid, have: (i) I saw her make a doll. (ii) They watched us play football. (iii) She hears him sing a song.
  4. After certain phrases such as: had better, had rather, would better, would rather: (i) I would better stay where I am. (ii) He would rather die than beg.
  5. After 'but', 'except' and 'than' such as: (i) She does nothing but complain. (ii) I would better talk than remain silent.
  6. In suggestions starting with why or why not: (i) Why talk to him? (ii) Why not go there and talk to him?

9.Participles — Definition & Types

A participle is a type of non-finite form of verb that does not follow subject-verb agreement in the sentence. Participles are the verbs that function as adjectives. They are also called verbal adjectives. There are three types of participles.

  1. Present Participle (first form of verb + 'ing' functioning as an adjective)
  2. Past Participle (third form of verb functioning as an adjective)
  3. Perfect Participle (having + third form of verb functioning as an adjective)

10.Use of Present Participles

We use a present participle (first form of verb + 'ing') to describe or modify a noun or a pronoun. A present participle can be placed before or after the noun or pronoun it modifies. Read the sentences given below to understand the use of present participles.

  • I will buy a jumping doll. (used as an adjective)
  • This building has no running water. (used as an adjective)
  • The lecture was interesting. (used as a subject complement)
  • We saw the moon rising. (used as an object complement)
  • She went away crying. (used as an adverb)
  • The person living on the first floor is my friend. (used as an adjective phrase)
  • Placing his hand on the Bible, he took the oath. (used to denote earlier action of the same subject)
  • It being a fine day, we arranged for a picnic. (used as an absolute phrase)

11.Use of Past Participle

We use a past participle (third form of verb) to describe or modify a noun or a pronoun. A past participle can be placed before or after the noun or pronoun it modifies. Read the sentences given below to understand the use of past participles.

  • A burnt child dreads the fire. (used as an adjective)
  • He appears bored. (used as a subject complement)
  • I found him tired. (used as an object complement)
  • The shop has been inaugurated now. (used in passive voice)
  • The man seems worried. (used as a subject complement)
  • They have finished their meal. (used in perfect tense)
  • The books read in childhood are forgotten. (used in an adjective phrase)
  • Deceived by his friends, he killed himself. (used in an adjective phrase)
  • The man left the hospital completely cured. (used as an adverb)

13.Use of Perfect Participle

We use a perfect participle (having + third form of verb) to denote an action which is complete and followed by another action.

  • Having completed my homework, I went to play.
  • Having found the correct answer, the boy was happy.
  • Having helped the poor, I felt overjoyed.
  • Having travelled for a long day, they felt tired.

15.Gerund — Definition & Use

A gerund is the form of the verb which ends in 'ing' and does the function of a noun. That is why the gerund is called a verbal noun.

  1. Swimming is a good exercise. (used as a subject)
  2. He likes reading books. (used as an object)
  3. My passion is teaching. (used as subject complement)
  4. I am fond of singing. (used after a preposition)

We use a gerund after the verbs given in the box below.

enjoy, consider, admit, allow, regret, keep, involve, imagine, fancy, risk, practise, postpone, stop, finish, resent, resume, delay, prefer, deny, detest, keep, worth, keep up, practise, quit, resist, miss, avoid, like, dread, suggest, forgive, look forward to, give up, put off, mind, can't help, couldn't help, need, no use, be used to, love etc.

17.Gerund after Preposition or Determiner

We also use a gerund after a preposition or a determiner.

  1. He is fond of playing cricket.
  2. Are you interested in going with us?
  3. You must switch off the lights and fans before leaving the room.
  4. Please don't mind my interrupting you.
  5. The printing of the books will be complete by the next month.

20.Quick Revision Checklist

  • Finite verbs change according to tense, number, mood or person; non-finite verbs do not.
  • There are three types of non-finite verbs: infinitives, participles, and gerunds.
  • An infinitive is the first form of the verb, used with 'to' (to-infinitive) or without 'to' (bare infinitive).
  • The bare infinitive follows do/does/did, modal auxiliaries, verbs like see/watch/hear/feel/let/make/help/bid/have, phrases like 'had better'/'had rather', 'but'/'except'/'than', and 'why'/'why not'.
  • A participle functions as an adjective (a verbal adjective). Present participle = verb + 'ing'; past participle = the third form of the verb; perfect participle = 'having' + third form, showing a completed action followed by another.
  • A gerund ends in 'ing' and functions as a noun (a verbal noun) — as subject, object, subject complement, or after a preposition/determiner.
  • Certain verbs take only an infinitive object, others take only a gerund object, and some (like begin, like, love, continue) can take either.
