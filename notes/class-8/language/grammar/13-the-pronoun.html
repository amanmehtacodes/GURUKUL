1.Warm-up — Why We Need Pronouns

Read the passage given below carefully.

Rohan and Rohan's father were at railway station, waiting for the train to Delhi. Rohan told Rohan's father that Rohan had forgotten to bring Rohan's camera. Rohan's father asked Rohan what Rohan had to do with Rohan's camera. Rohan told Rohan's father that Rohan wanted to click some photographs with Rohan's camera when Rohan and Rohan's father would visit the famous places at Delhi.

The repetition of the words 'Rohan' and 'Rohan's father' makes it rather awkward. Now read the revised version of the same passage with some words replaced.

Rohan and his father were at railway station, waiting for the train to Delhi. He told his father that he had forgotten to bring his camera. His father asked him what he had to do with his camera. He told his father that he wanted to click some photographs with his camera when they would visit the famous places at Delhi.

You can observe that the revised version of the passage seems refined as there is no repetition of the noun 'Rohan'. It has been replaced with the words 'he', 'his' or 'him'. Similarly, the nouns 'Rohan and Rohan's father' have been replaced with the word 'they'. These words that replace the nouns are called pronouns.

Definition: The word pronoun means 'used in place of noun'. A pronoun is a word which is used in place of a noun. Thus, a pronoun replaces a noun.

3.Personal Pronouns

Personal pronouns are the words used for persons or things. There are three types of personal pronouns.

  1. First Person Pronouns: 'I' and 'We' are the first person pronouns. These are used for the person/persons speaking. 'I' is used for one person whereas 'we' is used for many persons.
  2. Second Person Pronouns: 'You' is second person pronoun. It is used for the person/persons spoken to. 'You' has the same form for singular and plural pronouns.
  3. Third Person Pronouns: 'He', 'she', 'it' and 'they' are third person pronouns. These pronouns are used for the persons or things spoken of. 'He' is used to denote masculine gender, 'she' is used to denote feminine gender and 'it' is used for animals, non-living things and a baby when the gender is not specified. Although 'it' is used for non-living things, it is included in personal pronouns. Sometimes, 'it' is also called impersonal pronoun.

4.Personal Pronouns: Case

Read the following sentences carefully.

1. Me am going to market.   2. He helped I a lot.   3. Him is a good boy.   4. Their are playing cricket.
5. Mrs. Sharma is completing his work.   6. The students are doing his homework.   7. The cat licked her paw.   8. Smita washed its clothes.   9. You must do yours duty.

THINK: Do you observe anything awkward in the above sentences? The words in bold are all pronouns, but their form is not correct according to gender, number and their place and function in the sentence. (Corrected: I am going to market. He helped me a lot. He is a good boy. They are playing cricket. Mrs. Sharma is completing her work. The students are doing their homework. The cat licked its paw. Smita washed her clothes. You must do your duty.)

Just as nouns can be used as subjects, direct objects, indirect objects and to show possession in different sentences, pronouns can also be used in various ways. The use of pronouns in sentences decides their case. There are three cases of pronouns.

  1. Subjective Case (Subject Pronouns): A pronoun used as a subject is said to be in subjective case. Examples: He is a good boy. She is my elder sister. They are watching T.V. In the above examples, 'He', 'She' and 'They' are the doers of the action or verb. The predicate in each sentence tells us something about 'He', 'She' or 'They'. These pronouns are subjects in the sentences; hence their case is subjective case.
  2. Objective Case (Object Pronoun): A pronoun used as direct or indirect object is said to be in objective case. Examples: You helped me. We invited you. I will not disturb him. He gifted me a book. She asked him a question.
  3. Possessive Case (Possessive Pronoun): A pronoun that shows possession is said to be in possessive case. Example: This is his car. Or, This car is his.

Use of Possessive Pronouns as Possessive Adjectives

Both possessive pronouns and possessive adjectives are used to show possession or belonging. However, whereas pronouns replace the nouns, adjectives describe the nouns.

Examples: 1. This is my book. 2. This book is mine. In the examples given above, the words 'my' and 'mine' both show possession. However, the word 'my' is followed by the noun 'book'. It modifies the noun 'book'; hence it is called possessive adjective. On the other hand, the word 'mine' is used in place of the noun 'book'. It is called possessive pronoun.

5.Demonstrative Pronouns

Definition: The words 'this', 'that', 'these', 'those' are called demonstratives as they are used to point out to persons or things they refer to.

Look at the use of demonstrative pronouns in the following sentences.

  1. This is my pencil. (Used for singular thing which is near the speaker)
  2. That is your car. (Used for singular thing which is at a distance from the speaker)
  3. These are his kites. (Used for plural things which are near the speaker)
  4. These oranges are fresher than those we saw in the market. (Used for plural things which are at a distance from the speaker)

Demonstrative Pronouns and Demonstrative Adjectives

Both demonstrative pronouns and demonstrative adjectives are used to point out to a person or a thing they refer to. However, whereas demonstrative pronouns replace the nouns, demonstrative adjectives are placed before the nouns to describe the nouns.

Demonstrative PronounDemonstrative Adjective
This is my book.This book is mine.
That is your house.That house is yours.
These are their bags.These bags are theirs.
This is her pencil.This pencil is hers.

6.Interrogative Pronouns

Definition: The words 'what', 'which', 'who', 'whose' and 'whom' are interrogative pronouns. They are used to ask questions about persons or things.

Examples: 1. What are you doing here? 2. Who are these boys? 3. Which is your favourite colour? 4. Whom did you convey the message? 5. Whose is this house?

Important Note: 'Who', 'whom' and 'whose' are used for persons. 'What' is used for things. 'Which' is used for persons and things — it denotes selection of one or many out of the given options.

Example: Which of these dresses do you like the most?

Read the following sentences to understand the subjective, objective and possessive cases of interrogative pronouns.

  1. What is that big thing? — 'what' is the subject of the verb 'is'.
  2. What did you buy at market? — 'what' is the object of the verb 'buy'.
  3. Whose is this book? — 'whose' shows possession; hence it is in possessive case.

Interrogative Pronoun and Interrogative Adjective

Interrogative pronouns 'what', 'which' and 'whose' can also be used as interrogative adjectives. Interrogative adjectives are interrogative words followed immediately by a noun.

Interrogative PronounInterrogative Adjective

7.Relative Pronouns

Read the following pairs of sentences carefully and observe the use of pronouns.

  1. Who is this boy?
  2. This is the boy who helped me.
  3. Which is your coat?
  4. This is the coat which I bought at the market.

In examples 1 and 3, 'who' and 'which' are used to ask questions. In examples 2 and 4, 'who' and 'which' do not ask questions. They are used to join two parts of sentences.

Now examine how the following sentences are joined. This is the boy. The boy helped me. → This is the boy who helped me. (who = boy)

In the example given above, the word 'who' is used to replace the noun 'boy'. The word 'who' is called a pronoun. This pronoun also functions as a conjunction as it joins the two sentences in such a way that the pronoun used in one clause relates to the noun in the other. Therefore, it is called a relative pronoun. A noun that a relative pronoun refers to is called its antecedent. In the above example, 'who' is relative pronoun and 'boy' is its antecedent.

Examples:

I saw a man. His beard was very long. → I saw a man whose beard was very long. (Whose = man's)

That is the house. I bought it last month. → That is the house which I bought last month. (Which = house)

Forms and cases of relative pronouns
Subjective CaseObjective CasePossessive Case
For personswhowhomwhose
For thingswhat, thatwhat, that—
For persons or thingswhichwhich—

Read the following examples to understand the use and cases of relative pronouns:

  1. I met a boy. He was wearing a blue shirt. → I met a boy who was wearing a blue shirt. ('who' is the subject of 'wearing' — subjective case.)
  2. This is the student. The teacher gave him a prize. → This is the student whom the teacher gave a prize. ('whom' is the object of 'gave' — objective case.)
  3. This is the girl. Her father is a doctor. → This is the girl whose father is a doctor. ('whose' shows possession — possessive case.)
  4. This is the house. My father built it. → This is the house which my father built. ('which' is the object of 'built' — objective case.)
  5. All glitters. All is not gold. → All that glitters is not gold. ('that' is the subject of 'glitters' — subjective case.)
  6. This is it. I expect it of you. → This is what I expect of you. ('what' is the object of 'expect' — objective case.)

8.Reflexive & Emphatic Pronouns

Reflexive Pronouns

Compound personal pronouns are formed by adding the suffixes '-self' or '-selves' to personal pronouns. Examples: myself, ourselves, yourself, yourselves, himself, herself, itself and themselves.

A reflexive pronoun refers back to the subject and functions as an object in the sentence.

  1. The children fell down and hurt themselves.
  2. We enjoyed ourselves a lot at the party.
  3. She looked at herself in the mirror.
  4. Mr. Bhatia declared himself the new Managing Director of the company.

Emphatic Pronouns

When a compound personal pronoun is used only to lay emphasis on a noun or pronoun, it is called emphatic pronoun. It may be used just after the subject or at the end of the clause.

  1. The Principal himself declared the holiday.
  2. She cleaned the room herself.

The above two sentences can be rewritten by removing emphatic pronouns without affecting the meaning. Thus, emphatic pronouns are used only to lay emphasis. 1. The Principal declared the holiday. 2. She cleaned the room.

9.Indefinite Pronouns

Read the sentences given below carefully.

  1. Somebody is knocking at the door.
  2. None has the courage to tell the truth to the Principal.
  3. Everyone wants to be rich.
  4. Nobody is perfect in this world.
  5. One must do one's duty.
  6. Nothing can help me in this situation.
  7. I can do anything for you.
  8. Give me something to eat.

The bolded words in the sentences above do not refer to any specific or particular person or thing. These words refer to persons and things in general. These words are called indefinite pronouns.

Examples: one, none, all, any, many, several, both, others, much, few, some, someone, somebody, something, everybody, everyone, anyone, anybody, anything, nobody, nothing etc.

Important Notes: The words 'any', 'anyone', 'anybody' and 'anything' are usually used in negative sentences and interrogative sentences. 'One of' is always followed by a plural noun and takes a singular verb. Examples: 1. Do you need anything? 2. I can't find anybody in the room. 3. Has anyone seen my book? 4. One of my neighbours has gifted me this book.

10.Distributive Pronouns

Definition: The pronouns that refer to a single person or thing in a group, one at a time, are called distributive pronouns. Each, either, neither are called distributive pronouns. They are always singular and take a singular verb.

Each denotes every one of a number of persons or things in a group taken separately. Example: Each of the boys was given a blanket.

Either and neither are used while speaking about two persons or things. Either means one or the other of the two while neither means not one nor the other of the two. Examples: 1. Either of these two books can be purchased. Both are equally good. 2. Neither of these two roads leads to Delhi.

11.Reciprocal Pronouns

Definition: Pronouns that express a mutual or reciprocal relationship or action are called reciprocal pronouns. There are two pairs of reciprocal pronouns: 'each other', and 'one another'. 'Each other' is used when referring to two people. 'One another' is used when referring to more than two people.

Examples: 1. The two brothers love each other. 2. We must respect one another.

  25. Someone is knocking at the door. (Anyone → Someone, positive statement)
