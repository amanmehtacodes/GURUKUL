1.What Is a Conjunction?

Study carefully how the following pairs of sentences are joined.

  1. He bought apples. He bought mangoes.
    He bought apples and mangoes.
  2. My brother is tall. I am short.
    My brother is tall but I am short.
  3. He is ill. He cannot come to school.
    He is ill, so he cannot come to school.
    He cannot come to school because he is ill.

You can observe that in each pair of sentences given above, two different statements are given. These two different statements are joined by using the words 'and', 'but', 'so' and 'because'. These words which join two words or clauses are called conjunctions, connectors or linkers.

Conjunction: A word which is used to join two words, phrases or clauses is called a conjunction.

2.Kinds of Conjunction

1. Coordinative Conjunctions

A Coordinative Conjunction joins the grammatical units of the same type or equal status e.g. nouns with noun, adjective with adjective, phrase with phrase and clause with clause. It joins words, phrases or clauses of equal status. In a compound Sentence, it joins two coordinate Clauses.

Examples: and, but, for, or, still, yet, so, therefore, as well as, otherwise, else, only etc.

  • I went home. I changed my clothes.
    I went home and changed my clothes. (Joins verb with verb)
  • Take care of your health. You will fall ill.
    Take care of your health, otherwise you will fall ill. (Joins two coordinate clauses)
  • He likes ice-cream. He does not like juice.
    He likes ice-cream, but not juice. (Joins noun with noun)

2. Subordinating Conjunction

A subordinating conjunction joins subordinate clause/clauses to the principal clause in a complex sentence.

Examples: unless, until, because, since, as, if, whether, before, after, when, while, where, wherever etc.

  • I used to play cricket when I was a student.
  • He ran away because he was afraid.

The underlined clauses in the above sentences are Subordinate Clauses.

3. Correlative Conjunctions

Correlative conjunctions also join grammatical units of the same type or equal status e.g. nouns with noun, adjective with adjective, phrase with phrase and clause with clause. Correlative conjunctions are followed by the same part of speech. They are always used in pairs.

Examples: Both...and, Either...or, Neither...nor, Hardly...when, Scarcely...before, Not only...but also etc.

  • He is either a fool (noun) or a rogue (noun).
  • Hardly had he reached school (clause) when the peon rang the bell. (clause)

4. Compound Conjunctions

Compound conjunctions are groups of words that function as conjunctions. Some expressions or phrases act as conjunctions.

  • He will lend you money provided that you return it on time.
  • I will let you play in my garden on condition that you do not pluck the flowers.
  • The thief ran away as soon as he heard a noise.

These expressions or phrases are called Compound Conjunctions.

4.Types of Coordinative Conjunctions — Cumulative or Copulative Conjunctions

Cumulative conjunctions add or connect words, phrases or clauses of the same type, status or level. They are used to convey the idea of similarity between the units being joined.

Examples: and, also, too, as well as, both...and, not only...but also etc.

Read the pair of sentences given below.

He is smart. He is intelligent.

In the above pair of sentences, we come to know about two qualities of 'He'. Since both the qualities are positive and don't show any contradiction, we will join the above pair of sentences using a cumulative conjunction.

  1. He is smart and intelligent.
  2. He is smart as well as intelligent.
  3. He is smart; and intelligent too.
  4. He is both smart and intelligent.
  5. He is smart; and intelligent also.
  6. He is not only smart but also intelligent.

5.Types of Coordinative Conjunctions — Adversative Conjunctions

These conjunctions are used to connect or join two contradictory, contrasting or opposite ideas.

Examples: but, still, however, nevertheless, only, whereas, yet

Read the pair of sentences given below.

He is intelligent. He is not hard working.
He is intelligent, but not hardworking.

  • He is poor but honest.
  • He was weak; still he managed to walk for long.
  • He lost everything in life; however he started his life anew with a strong will.
  • Everyone in the family opposed him; nevertheless he chased his dreams.
  • Do what you like, only don't disturb me.
  • My friend is tall whereas his brother is not (tall).
  • He has a lot of wealth, yet he is a miser.

6.Types of Coordinative Conjunctions — Disjunctive or Alternative Conjunction

These conjunctions express a choice between two words, phrases or clauses.

Examples: or, nor, either...or, neither...nor, otherwise, else

  • Run fast, or you will lose the race.
  • You can either wait or go.
  • He is neither intelligent nor hardworking.
  • Work hard, otherwise you will fail.
  • Return these books on time, else you will be fined.

7.Types of Coordinative Conjunctions — Illative Conjunctions

When a fact or statement is inferred or provided from another statement, the two of them are joined by using an illative coordinating conjunction.

Examples: so, hence, therefore, for, then

  • My foot was injured, so I could not walk.
  • It had been raining since morning; hence we could not go out.
  • He is not well today; therefore he cannot come to school.
  • He missed the train, for he was late.
  • We are getting late, let's hurry up then.

8.Important Note: The Acronym FANBOYS

Remember the Acronym FANBOYS to remember some important conjunctions.

F = for
A = and, as well as
N = neither...nor
B = but
O = or, otherwise, else
Y = yet, still
S = so, hence, therefore

10.Exercise: Join the following pairs of sentences using the conjunctions given in the brackets.

Rewrite each pair as one joined sentence using the given conjunction.

  1. They were late. They managed to catch the train. (however)
  2. He is underweight. He cannot participate in this competition. (therefore)
  3. I like chocolate. I like cake. (both...and)
  4. Wear a sweater. You will get cold. (else)

Sample Answers

  2. He is underweight; therefore, he cannot participate in this competition.
  3. I like both chocolate and cake.
  4. Wear a sweater, else you will get cold.
  5. He worked hard; nevertheless, he could not qualify for the post.
  6. Either he has solved the sum or his brother has solved the sum.
  7. The weather was cold and wet; still, we had a great time.
  8. Deposit your fee by tomorrow, or your name will be struck off.
  9. He does not exercise regularly; hence, he has put on weight.
  10. He is neither a producer nor a director.

15.Types of Subordinating Conjunctions — Time

Subordinating conjunctions may be divided according to their meaning or function.

Examples: Before, as, after, when, while, since, as soon as, till, until, so long as, as long as, now that, once etc.

  • Look before you leap.
  • I will go to play after I have finished my work.
  • I used to play cricket when I was a student.
  • I haven't seen her since she left her job.
  • Wait here till I come.

16.Types of Subordinating Conjunctions — Place

Examples: Where, wherever, whence

  • He will follow you wherever you go.
  • I kept the books where you told me to keep.
  • They returned whence they had come.

17.Types of Subordinating Conjunctions — Manner or Extent

Examples: as, so far as, as if, as though, as far as etc.

  • They worked as I had instructed.
  • So far as his health is concerned, he is better now.
  • As far as his behaviour is concerned, he has shown improvement.
  • He walked as if he were lame.
  • It sounds as though you had a good time.

18.Types of Subordinating Conjunctions — Cause

Examples: Because, since, as

  • He died because he could not get proper treatment.
  • I will lead this project since you want me to do it.

19.Types of Subordinating Conjunctions — Condition

Examples: Provided, provided that, on condition that, if, in case, unless, supposing

  • You can stand first in the class provided you work hard.
  • You can stand first in the class provided that you work hard.
  • You can stand first in the class if you work hard.
  • You will not stand first in the class unless you work hard.

20.Types of Subordinating Conjunctions — Result or Effect

Examples: so...that

  • He is so weak that he cannot walk.

21.Types of Subordinating Conjunctions — Purpose

Examples: that, so that, in order that, lest...should, for fear that

  • He worked hard so that he might pass the examination.
  • He is working hard in order that he may pass the examination.
  • Run fast lest you should miss the train.
  • We eat that we may live.

22.Types of Subordinating Conjunctions — Contrast or Concession

Examples: though, although, notwithstanding, as, even if, whether, however, whatever, while, when

  • Though he is poor, he is honest. He is honest though he is poor.
  • Although he is poor, he is honest. He is honest although he is poor.
  • You have to eat it whether you like it or not.
  • Whatever happens, I will fight till the end.
  • While he is good at science, his brother is hopeless.

23.Types of Subordinating Conjunctions — Comparison

Examples: than, as

  • My brother is taller than I am.
  • Ramesh ran faster than Suresh (did).

24.Correlative Conjunctions

#PairSense / UseExample
1Both...andUsed to join similar things/conceptsHe is both a lyricist and composer.
2Either...orUsed to choose/select either of two choicesYou can take either a bus or a train for Delhi.
3Neither...norUsed to reject either of the two choicesHe is neither a doctor nor a lawyer.
4Hardly...whenUsed in the sense of 'as soon as'Hardly had I stepped out of my house when it started raining.
5Scarcely...when/beforeUsed in the sense of 'as soon as'I scarcely had time to ring the bell before the door opened. He had scarcely reached school when the peon rang the bell.
6Not only...but alsoUsed in the sense of 'and' or 'in addition to'He not only invited me, but also gave me a beautiful gift.
7No sooner...thanUsed in the sense of 'as soon as'No sooner did the thief see the police than he ran away. No sooner had I reached home than it started raining.
8Though...yetUsed to express contrastThough the baby is weak yet it is active. / Though the baby is weak, it is active.
9Although...yetUsed to express contrastAlthough he worked hard yet he could not succeed. / Although he worked hard, he could not succeed.
10So...thatUsed to show result or effectHe is so intelligent that he can solve these sums easily.
11Whether...orUsed to join two contrasting ideasYou should work under him, whether you like him or not.
12Lest...shouldUsed in the sense of 'otherwise'Walk fast lest you should miss the train.

26.Exercise: Rewrite the following sentences by correcting the errors.

Rewrite each sentence, correcting the conjunction error.

  1. He is a poet but novelist.
  2. She is very beautiful and proud.
  3. I have not met her when she left the office.
  4. Make hay before the sun shines.
  5. You cannot succeed if you work hard.
  6. Either John nor his brother has broken the window.
  7. The tea is very hot that I cannot drink it.
  8. Follow the rules and you will be terminated.
  9. He is rich and arrogant.
  10. He did not come to school, so he was ill.
  11. Rajat is not only hardworking but loyal.
  12. She is neither studying or playing.
  13. I do not know that when she will go.
  14. Walk carefully lest you must fall.
  15. You will not catch the train unless you do not hurry up.
  16. Wait here after the train arrives.
  17. It was raining heavily so that he was late.
  18. The little child was lost in the forest and he did not lose heart.
  19. I got the book when I left it.
  20. She cannot read nor write.
Answer Key
  1. He is both a poet and a novelist. ('but' wrongly joins two similar, non-contrasting roles — use 'both...and')
  2. She is very beautiful but proud. (beauty and vanity contrast, so 'and' should be 'but')
  3. I have not met her since she left the office. ('when' cannot mark a stretch of time up to now — use 'since')
  4. Make hay while the sun shines. (the idiom uses 'while', not 'before')
  5. You cannot succeed unless you work hard. ('if' reverses the meaning — needs the negative-condition 'unless')
  6. Neither John nor his brother has broken the window. ('either' cannot pair with 'nor' — needs 'neither')
  7. The tea is so hot that I cannot drink it. ('very...that' is not a correlative pair — needs 'so...that')
  8. Follow the rules, or you will be terminated. ('and' wrongly implies obeying causes termination — needs 'or')
  9. He is rich but arrogant. (wealth and arrogance contrast, so 'and' should be 'but')
  10. He did not come to school because he was ill. ('so' reverses cause and effect — needs 'because')
  11. Rajat is not only hardworking but also loyal. ('but' must be completed with 'also' in this correlative pair)
  12. She is neither studying nor playing. ('or' cannot pair with 'neither' — needs 'nor')
  13. I do not know when she will go. ('that' is redundant before the question word 'when')
  14. Walk carefully lest you should fall. ('lest' is completed by 'should', not 'must')
  15. You will not catch the train unless you hurry up. ('unless' already carries the negative — a second 'not' cancels the meaning)
  16. Wait here till/until the train arrives. ('after' contradicts waiting — needs 'till'/'until')
  17. It was raining heavily, so he was late. ('so that' expresses purpose, not simple result — needs plain 'so')
  18. The little child was lost in the forest, but he did not lose heart. ('and' should be 'but' to show contrast with the danger just described)
  19. I got back the book that I had left. (the original is illogical — 'when' does not show what was recovered)
  20. She can neither read nor write. ('cannot...nor' is not a valid pair — needs 'neither...nor', or 'cannot read or write')

27.Quick Revision Checklist

  • A conjunction joins two words, phrases or clauses.
  • There are four kinds: Coordinative, Subordinating, Correlative, and Compound conjunctions.
  • Coordinative conjunctions split further into Cumulative, Adversative, Disjunctive, and Illative — remember FANBOYS for the core set.
  • Subordinating conjunctions are grouped by function: Time, Place, Manner/Extent, Cause, Condition, Result, Purpose, Contrast/Concession, and Comparison.
  • Correlative conjunctions always work in fixed pairs — both...and, either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also, and more.
  • Watch for the classic errors: mismatched pairs (either...nor), double negatives (unless...not), and 'that' stacked in front of a question word (that when).
