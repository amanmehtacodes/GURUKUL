### Level 2: Medium (Q51–Q100) — Full Solutions

**Q51.** 196 = 2×2×7×7 = 2²×7² = (2×7)² = 14² → **Perfect square; √196 = 14**

**Q52.** 288 = 2×2×2×2×2×3×3 = 2⁵×3². The 2's exponent (5) is odd → **Not a perfect square**

**Q53.** 512 = 2×2×2×2×2×2×2×2×2 = 2⁹ = (2³)³ = 8³ → **Perfect cube; ∛512 = 8**

**Q54.** 360 = 2×2×2×3×3×5 = 2³×3²×5¹. The 3 and 5 exponents aren't multiples of 3 → **Not a perfect cube**

**Q55.** 4 trailing zeros → square has **8 trailing zeros**. Since 4 is even, this number CAN potentially be a perfect square (zero-count alone doesn't guarantee it, but it passes this test).

**Q56.** A cube's trailing zeros = 3k (multiple of 3) where k = trailing zeros of original number. 4 is not a multiple of 3, so **no cube can end in exactly 4 zeros**.

**Q57.** Check units digits: 2222 (ends 2 → not square), 3249 (ends 9 → possible), 5678 (ends 8 → not square), 7921 (ends 1 → possible). **Answer: 2222 and 5678 are NOT perfect squares** (by units digit rule).

### Level 4: Critical Thinking (Q131–Q150) — Guided Solutions

**Q131.** For any non-square number N, every factor f pairs with a DIFFERENT factor N/f (since f ≠ N/f when N isn't a perfect square). This creates distinct pairs (f, N/f), and since every factor belongs to exactly one such pair, the total factor count is always **even** (pairs × 2). Only when f = N/f (i.e., f² = N, meaning N is a perfect square) does a factor pair collapse into a single unpaired factor, making the count odd.

**Q132.** If Person k only toggles multiples of k greater than k (i.e., 2k, 3k, 4k...), then locker n is toggled once for every factor of n EXCEPT n=1 toggling itself is excluded, and more importantly every factor k of n where k<n toggles it, but k=n (person n toggling locker n itself) is excluded too. This effectively removes one toggle (from person n) from every locker, changing parity: previously-open lockers (squares, odd factor count) become closed, and previously-closed lockers (non-squares, even count) become open. **The open lockers would now be the non-square numbers.**

**Q133.** Sum of first n odd numbers = 1+3+5+...+(2n−1). This is an arithmetic series with first term a=1, last term l=2n−1, and n terms. Sum = n/2 × (a+l) = n/2 × (1+2n−1) = n/2 × 2n = **n²**. This proves the identity for all n.

**Q134.** (n+1)² − n² = n²+2n+1−n² = 2n+1. Since 2n+1 counts the (n+1)² value itself as one of the numbers, the numbers strictly BETWEEN n² and (n+1)² number (2n+1)−1 = **2n**. This matches the pattern observed (e.g., n=16 gives 32).

**Q135.** Following the pattern where the constant for n² appears at level 2 (value 2 = 2!) and n³ at level 3 (value 6 = 3!), the pattern suggests n⁴ would stabilise at **Level 4** with a constant value of **4! = 24**. (This follows the general calculus/finite-differences result that the kth differences of nᵏ are constant and equal to k!.)

**Q136.** For N to be a perfect square, every prime's exponent in its factorisation must be divisible by 2. For N to also be a perfect cube, every exponent must be divisible by 3. The only exponents satisfying BOTH conditions are multiples of LCM(2,3)=**6**. Hence N must be a perfect 6th power (n⁶) for some integer n.

**Q137.** Checking whether a number is NOT a taxicab number requires testing EVERY possible pair of cubes (a³,b³) with a³+b³ ≤ N to see if more than one pair sums to N. As N grows, the number of possible pairs (a,b) grows roughly proportional to N^(2/3), making exhaustive checking computationally expensive — this is why taxicab numbers become progressively rarer and harder to find as N increases (this is related to real research in number theory, e.g., Ramanujan's own further work on "Ramanujan numbers" for sums of three cubes).

**Q138.** Write N = M × 10^k, where M is NOT divisible by 10 (i.e., M's last digit isn't 0). Then N² = M² × 10^(2k). Since M isn't divisible by 10, M² also isn't divisible by 10 (as 10=2×5, and if M lacks a factor of 2 or 5, M² still lacks it as a factor to make it divisible by 10 completely — more precisely, the power of 10 dividing N² is exactly 2k). Hence N² has exactly **2k trailing zeros**.

**Q139.** Using the same setup, N = M × 10^k. N³ = M³ × 10^(3k). Since M isn't divisible by 10, M³ also retains that property, so the exact power of 10 dividing N³ is 3k. Hence N³ has exactly **3k trailing zeros**.

**Q140.** (10−d)² = 100−20d+d². Since 100 and 20d are both multiples of 10, they don't affect the units digit — only d² determines the units digit of (10−d)². This is the SAME d² term that determines the units digit of d² itself. Hence d and (10−d) always produce squares with the same units digit (e.g., d=3: 3²=9 and 7²=49, both end in 9).

**Q141.** Assume for contradiction that n is odd. Then n=2k+1 for some integer k, so n² = 4k²+4k+1, which is odd (as shown in Q123). This contradicts our assumption that n² is even. Therefore, our assumption was false, and **n must be even**. (Proof by contradiction.)

**Q142.** Counterexample: **12 is divisible by 4 but is NOT a perfect square** (12=4×3, not a perfect square since 3 remains unpaired). Correct conclusion: perfect squares that are even MUST be divisible by 4 (i.e., if n is even, n² is always divisible by 4), but the converse (divisible by 4 ⟹ perfect square) is FALSE.

**Q143.** Tₙ + Tₙ₊₁ = n(n+1)/2 + (n+1)(n+2)/2 = (n+1)[n+(n+2)]/2 = (n+1)(2n+2)/2 = (n+1)×2(n+1)/2 = **(n+1)²**. This proves the identity algebraically for all n.

**Q144.** This "halving the interval" method mirrors **binary search**, a computer science algorithm that repeatedly splits a search range in half to find a target value in O(log n) time, rather than checking each value one by one (O(n) time). This is why bracketing + refining converges to the answer much faster than sequential checking, especially for large numbers.

**Q145.** 50²×2 = 2×50² = 5000. 50³/25 = 50³/25 = (50×50×50)/25 = 50×50×2 = 5000. Using exponent reasoning: 50³/25 = 50³/50^(log₅₀25)... more simply, 25 = 50²/100, so 50³/25 = 50³×100/50² = 50×100 = 5000. **Both are equal (5000 = 5000)** — an elegant equivalence discoverable through exponent manipulation before brute-force calculating.

**Q146.** First odd number of the block for n³ = n²−n+1. Test n=4: 16−4+1=13 → block 13,15,17,19, sum=64=4³ ✓. Test n=7: 49−7+1=43 → block 43,45,47,49,51,53,55, sum=343=7³ ✓. Formula confirmed: **first term = n² − n + 1**.

**Q147.** A square root can be thought of as the "origin" or "source" from which a square number "grows" — just as a plant's root is the source from which the visible plant grows upward. The number under the root symbol is like the "fruit" (the square), and the square root is the hidden "root" that produced it. This metaphor of source/origin naturally maps the biological concept of a root to the mathematical concept of the base value that generates a square through multiplication.

**Q148.** Investigating squares 1-200 against cubes by checking each systematically: squares are 1,4,9,16,25,36,49,64,81,100,121,144,169,196 and cubes are 1,8,27,64,125. Testing each square's digit-reversal: most give non-cube results, **except 100**, whose digits reversed give **1**, and 1 = 1³ is indeed a perfect cube. So **100 (square) reverses to 1 (cube)** is a valid match within the range (note: single-digit numbers like 1, 4, 9 are trivially both squares under reversal since they're palindromes, so 100→1 is the first genuinely interesting case). Students should show their systematic checking table as part of the answer.

**Q149.** Three consecutive integers n, n+1, n+2 rarely share compatible prime factor structures because consecutive integers are always coprime (gcd=1) with their immediate neighbor, meaning their prime factorisations don't overlap in ways that could combine into a clean cube. Example: 3×4×5=60=2²×3×5 — the exponents (2,1,1) aren't multiples of 3, so 60 isn't a cube. Since one of any two consecutive numbers introduces a "new" prime or a mismatched exponent, the product structurally resists forming three matched groups needed for a perfect cube (except in degenerate cases like 0×1×2=0, which is a cube trivially since 0³=0).
