Read the following forms of the verbs carefully:
  Æ Go, goes, went, gone, going, to go, having gone
  Æ Look, looks, looked, looked, looking, to look, having looked
  Æ Help, helps, helped, helped, helping, to help, having helped
  Æ Be, is, am, are, was, were, to be, been, being, having been
  Æ Do, does, did, done, doing, to do, having done
  Æ Has, have, had, to have, having, having had
Think: Why do we use different forms of verbs in sentences?
Look at the changes in the forms of verbs in the following sentences:
    1. He goes to school to study.
        They go to school to study. (Change in number of the subject)
    2. I will help you. (Determination)
        I may help you. (Possibility) (Change in expression/mood)
    3. They like swimming.
        They liked swimming. (Change in tense)
    4. He ﬁnds the door locked.
        I ﬁnd the door locked. (Change in person of the subject)
The verbs in blue font in the above sentences are called ﬁnite verbs or ﬁnite forms of verbs because these verbs
change their forms according to change in tense, number, mood or person. The verbs in italic font don't change
their forms according to change in tense, number, mood or person. These verbs are called non-ﬁnite forms of
verbs.

                                                 Infinitives
An inﬁnitive is a type of non-ﬁnite form of verb that does not follow subject-verb agreement in the
sentence. It is the ﬁrst form of verb used with ‘to’ or without ‘to’.

There are two types of Inﬁnitives.
    1. Inﬁnitive with 'to': 'to+ ﬁrst form of verb'
Examples:
        (a) He goes to school to study.     (b) I wanted to play.           (c) They wish to join a new school.
In the above sentences, the verbs, 'to study', 'to play' and 'to join' are inﬁnitives with 'to'.
    2. Inﬁnitive without 'to' (Bare Inﬁnitive): First form of verb
Examples:
        (a) Please let me go.         (b) I will help you.            (c) She does nothing but complain.
In the above sentences, the verbs, 'go', 'help' and 'complain' are inﬁnitives without 'to' or bare inﬁnitives.

                                  Infinitive without ‘to’ (Bare Infinitive)
Bare Inﬁnitive is used in the following cases:
(1) After the auxiliary 'do', 'does' or 'did':
        (i) He does not like mangoes. (ii) They did not stay here. (iii) Do you have a spare pen?
(2) After modal auxiliaries:
        (i) I will help you. (ii) He may come tomorrow. (iii) You need not go there.
 (3) After certain verbs such as: see, watch, hear, feel, let, make, help, bid, have:
        (i) I saw her make a doll. (ii) They watched us play football. (iii) She hears him sing a song.
 (4) After certain phrases such as: had better, had rather, would better, would rather
        (i) I would better stay where I am. (ii) He would rather die than beg.
 (5) After 'but', 'except' and 'than' such as:
        (i) She does nothing but complain. (ii) I would better talk than remain silent.
(6) In suggestions starting with why or why not:
        (i) Why talk to him? (ii) Why not go there and talk to him?

                                                              Participles
A participle is a type of non-ﬁnite form of verb that does not follow subject-verb agreement in the sentence.
Participles are the verbs that function as adjectives. They are also called verbal adjectives. There are three
types of participles.
     (i) Present Participle ( ﬁrst form of verb + 'ing' functioning as an adjective)
     (ii) Past Participle ( third form of verb functioning as an adjective)
     (iii) Perfect Participle ( having + third form of verb functioning as an adjective)
                                               Use of Present Participles
We use a present participle (ﬁrst form of verb+ ‘ing’) to describe or modify a noun or a pronoun. A present
participle can be placed before or after the noun or pronoun it modiﬁes. Read the sentences given below to
understand the use of present participles.
     Æ    I will buy a jumping doll. (used as an adjective)
     Æ    This building has no running water. (used as an adjective)
     Æ    The lecture was interesting. (used as a subject complement)
     Æ    We saw the moon rising. (used as an object complement)
     Æ    She went away crying. (used as an adverb)
     Æ    The person living on the ﬁrst ﬂoor is my friend. (used as an adjective phrase)
     Æ    Placing his hand on the Bible, he took the oath. (used to denote earlier action of the same subject)
     Æ    It being a ﬁne day, we arranged for a picnic. (used as an absolute phrase)
                                                   Use of Past Participle
We use a past participle (third form of verb) to describe or modify a noun or a pronoun. A past participle
can be placed before or after the noun or pronoun it modiﬁes. Read the sentences given below to
understand the use of past participles.
     Æ    A burnt child dreads the ﬁre. (used as an adjective)
     Æ    He appears bored. (used as a subject complement)
     Æ    I found him tired. (used as an object complement)
     Æ    The shop has been inaugurated now. (used in passive voice)
     Æ    The man seems worried. (used as a subject complement)
     Æ    They have ﬁnished their meal. (used in perfect tense)
     Æ    The books read in childhood are forgotten. (used in an adjective phrase)
     Æ    Deceived by his friends, he killed himself. (used in an adjective Phrase)
     Æ    The man left the hospital completely cured. (used as an adverb)

                                                                   Gerund
A gerund is the form of the verb which ends in ‘ing’ and does the function of a noun. That is why; the
gerund is called a verbal noun.
Examples:
   1. Swimming is a good exercise. (used as a subject)
   2. He likes reading books. (used as an object)
   3. My passion is teaching. (used as subject complement)
   4. I am fond of singing. (used after a preposition)
