Chapter-10                                        Determiners
Deﬁnition: A determiner is a word which announces the coming of a noun in a sentence. It is used before a
noun to qualify or modify it. Most of adjectives function as determiners because they are used before nouns to
describe them. An adjective used after a noun is not considered to be a determiner.

                                                         Articles
Read the following sentences carefully.
    1. I have an imported pen.          2.      The pen has a golden cap.
    3. The cap has a pin.               4.      The pin has an automatic button.
In the above sentences, the words 'a', 'an' and 'the' are called articles.
There are two types of articles:
    1. Indeﬁnite Articles: 'A' and 'an' are called indeﬁnite articles.
    2. Deﬁnite Article: 'The' is called deﬁnite article.
                                               Use of ‘A’ and ‘An’
Both 'a' and 'an' are used to denote a countable singular noun. 'A' is used before a noun which starts with a
consonant sound whereas 'an' is used before a noun which starts with a vowel sound.
                                    Vowel and Consonant Sounds
There are 26 letters in English alphabet. Generally, out of 26 letters 'a', 'e', 'i', 'o' and 'u' are considered to be vowels
and the remaining 21 letters are considered to be consonants. In fact, these 26 letters used individually or in
combinations, produce 44 sounds. Thus, there are 44 sounds in English language. Out of these 44 sounds, there
are 20 vowel sounds and 24 consonant sounds. 'A', 'e', 'i', 'o' and 'u' are letters frequently used to start a word with a
vowel sound, hence, often labelled as vowels. While selecting the article 'a' or 'an' for a singular noun, it is
important to note whether the noun starts with a vowel sound or a consonant sound. It is not the ﬁrst letter which
decides the sound; it is the pronunciation of the word that decides the sound.

                             Adjectives of Number as Determiners

An adjective of number, when used as a determiner, describes the number of a noun. It answers the question, 'how
many'. It is used for countable nouns.
There are two types of adjectives of number.
(i) Deﬁnite Numeral Adjectives: Deﬁnite numeral adjectives indicate the exact number of a noun.
Example: Five girls are playing in the park.
There are two types of deﬁnite numeral adjectives.
(a) Cardinals: Cardinals denote the exact number of a noun.
Example: There are ten students in this row.
(b) Ordinals: Ordinals denote the order or rank of a noun. Examples: ﬁrst, second, third, tenth, last, next etc.
Article 'the' or a possessive determiner is used before an ordinal.
Examples: The third boy in this row is my friend. 2. Pt. J.L. Nehru was the ﬁrst Prime Minister of India.
(ii) Indeﬁnite Numeral Adjectives: An indeﬁnite numeral adjectives does't indicate the exact number of the
noun it refers to.
Examples: 1. Several pages of this book are torn.                   2. There are many monkeys on this tree.
                            Adjectives of Quantity as Determiners
An adjective of quantity indicates the quantity of a noun. It answers the question, 'how much'. It is used before
uncountable nouns.
Examples: some, any, no, much, little, half, all, whole, sufﬁcient, enough, great, etc.
   1. I ate some rice.
   2. There is not much oil in the bottle.
   3. I don't have any money.
   4. This statement makes no sense.
   5. They have sufﬁcient money to buy a house.
   6. I don't have enough time to waste on these matters.
   7. There was little water in the jug.
   8. The crow drank the little water which was in the jug.
                      Possessive Adjectives as Possessive Determiners
The words 'my', 'our', 'your', 'his', 'her', 'its' and 'their' are used as possessive adjectives or possessive determiners.
They show belonging or possession.

             Demonstrative Adjectives as Demonstrative Determiners
The words 'this', 'that', 'these', 'those' and 'such' are called demonstratives as they are used to point out to persons

demonstrative determiners.

                      Distributive Adjectives as Determiners
The adjectives that refer to a single person or thing in a group, one at a time, are called distributive adjectives.
‘each’, ‘every’, ‘either’, ‘neither’ are called distributive adjectives. Distributive adjectives are used as
determiners before nouns.
                                            Each and Every
‘Each’ and ‘every’ are similar in meaning, but there is a difference between the two. Each is used when speaking
of two or more persons or things. 'Every' is used when speaking of more than two persons or things. Each directs
attention to every person or thing of a group individually and separately. 'Every' is comprehensive, considering
all; it directs attention to the whole group without any exception. ‘Each’ is used to denote a person or thing out of a
known or limited group. 'Every' is used in a wide sense to denote persons or things when the number is unlimited.
Examples:
     1. Each participant was given a certiﬁcate. (considering each individual out of a limited group)
     2. Every man wants to be rich. (considering all in an unlimited group)

                                           Either and Neither
Either' has two meanings: (i) One out of two (ii) Each of two
'Neither' means none out of two.
Examples:
    1. There are trees on either side of the road. (each out of two)
    2. Neither side of the road has trees. (None out of two)

                                             Little, a little, the little
Little, a little, the little are used for uncountable nouns as they are quantitative determiners. There is a difference
in the use of these three.
Little: ‘Little’ is used to express a quantity which is very small, or almost negligible. ‘Little’ means almost
nothing or hardly any. It is used to convey a negative sense.
A little: ‘A little’ means small in quantity but at least some. It conveys a positive sense.
The little: ‘The little’ means small in quantity, but the whole of it. It is used to consider the whole however little
may be the quantity.
                                                  Few, a few, the few
Few, a few, the few are used for countable nouns as they are numeral determiners, but there is a difference in the
use of these three.
Few: 'Few' is used to express a number which is very small, or almost negligible. 'Few' means almost none or
hardly any. It is used to convey a negative sense.
A few: ‘A few’ means small in number but at least some. It conveys a positive sense.
The few: ‘The few’ means small in number, but all of them. It is used to consider all however few may be the
number.

‘Elder’ and ‘eldest’ are used only for persons. Elder is never followed by ‘than’. It is followed by ‘to’.
